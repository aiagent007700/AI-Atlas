# Industry voices and evidence

A living tutorial should feel connected to the world outside the textbook. It should also avoid turning announcements into proof.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer sources in this order when possible:

1. Research papers and technical reports
2. Standards and regulatory publications
3. Operator, customer, or deployment evidence
4. Official engineering documentation
5. Conference talks and technical presentations
6. Analyst interpretation and reputable reporting
7. Marketing material and social discussion

Lower-ranked sources can be useful for discovery, but important claims should be checked against stronger evidence.

## Quote protocol

A quote should include:

* Speaker and role
* Organization
* Date
* Source title and link
* A short surrounding-context note
* Whether the statement is an observation, claim, forecast, or opinion

Do not use a quote merely because it sounds memorable. Use it because it reveals a meaningful assumption, trade-off, deployment lesson, or disagreement.

## The evidence card

Each major update should be summarized with:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Signal | What changed |
| Source | Where the evidence comes from |
| Evidence type | Research, standard, deployment, announcement, or interpretation |
| Confidence | High, medium, or limited |
| Tutorial link | Which durable concept it updates |
| Open question | What remains uncertain |

## Avoiding hype

Ask:

* Is this a demonstrated capability or a stated intention?
* Is the result measured on a benchmark or in a live environment?
* Is the deployment production, pilot, prototype, or concept?
* Are the costs, failure modes, and limits reported?
* Does an independent source corroborate the claim?

## Thought experiment

If a company reports that an AI system “transformed operations” but provides no baseline, metric, scope, or failure data, what exactly have we learned?
